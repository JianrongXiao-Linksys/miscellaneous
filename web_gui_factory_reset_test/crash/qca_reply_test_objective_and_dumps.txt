1. TEST OBJECTIVE

It is the first of your two options: overall endurance and stability across repeated factory resets, including CNSS/WLAN bring-up. It is not a study of how individual modules or drivers load during initialization.

Background: the loop mirrors a manufacturing-line sequence. Each unit is factory reset and then re-provisioned, and it has to come back with the management interface and WLAN fully operational before the unit is accepted. The automated test reproduces that sequence back to back:

  1. trigger a factory reset through the device management API
  2. wait for the unit to go offline (up to 150 s)
  3. wait for it to answer ping again (up to 180 s)
  4. wait a fixed 10 s grace period
  5. HTTPS GET of the factory status endpoint, expecting "Idle"
  6. a management API request on port 80
  7. as soon as those answer, trigger the next reset

15 to 30 cycles per run. The pass/fail criterion is end to end: the unit must survive reset -> reboot -> full initialization (which includes CNSS and WLAN bring-up) -> services responding. We do not measure module load ordering or init timing, and we never load or unload drivers by hand.

One detail of the timing is worth calling out, because it is deliberate and it is likely relevant to you: step 4 waits only 10 s, and step 7 fires the next reset as soon as the two checks answer. We do not wait for initialization to complete. A reset can therefore arrive while CNSS and WLAN bring-up are still in progress. That is intentional, because it is what the production station does.

We also run a second variant that follows the procedure you recommended earlier in this case: 15 iterations, SSH and HTTP/HTTPS confirmed on every cycle, and the kernel log scanned for overlayfs and -116 after each reset. The two panics reported here came from the production-timing variant above.

The CNSS panic is not what this test was written to find. It surfaced as a side effect, and we are reporting it because the assert is fatal and the unit reboots.

One correlation that may help you scope it: both panics in the last capture occurred on boots where the device configuration tree was empty or unreadable. The console shows a flood of "uci: Entry not found", "uci: I/O error", "Region= not found in list." and an empty SKU string before each panic. With no valid region/SKU/board parameters, board-data and regulatory selection have nothing valid to select, the CNSS error path is entered, and the assert fires. A factory reset, or a reset that does not complete cleanly, is what produces that state, which is why a reset loop is an effective trigger. From our point of view the reset loop is only the trigger; the defect is that CNSS calls CNSS_ASSERT() on these paths instead of returning an error to its caller.

That distinction matters most for the second panic, because it is reached from userspace:

  nl80211_vendor_cmd -> wlan_cfg80211_set_wificonfiguration -> wlan_cfg80211_set_params -> ol_ath_ucfg_setparam -> ol_ath_check_and_start_target -> ol_ath_target_start -> ol_ath_soc_start -> __cnss_subsystem_get -> BUG() at main.c:3609

A normal userspace configuration command should not be able to panic the kernel, regardless of how the device got into that state.
